## Decisions

- **Source: Edelman et al. 2009** (already in `sources/`), the paper named in the README's "Body weight" extension. It reports by **BMI**, not weight, so the page asks for **weight + height** and works out BMI. Units: kg/cm or lb/ft-in.
- **What changes with BMI: clearance only.** Edelman: levonorgestrel half-life 25.6 h (normal BMI, mean 21.9) vs. 52.1 h (obese, mean 37.3), volume of distribution and SHBG not different. Same volume and twice the half-life gives clearance × 25.6/52.1 = 0.49. In the model this scales `k10`.
- **Between and beyond the two groups**: clearance 1 up to BMI 21.9, 0.49 from 37.3 up (Edelman's BMI ≥ 40 subgroup looked like the rest of the obese group), smooth in between (log of clearance linear in BMI). The label studies are taken to be normal-BMI women.
- **Minipill**: no BMI data exist for norethindrone. Same change applied, labelled on the page as an untested assumption.
- **Match clearance, not half-life**: scaling `k10` by 0.49 raises levonorgestrel's model half-life ×1.75 (34 → 60 h), not ×2.03. One rate can't fix both in two compartments; clearance sets total exposure.
- **Website stays precomputed**: Python exports a grid of 9 BMIs (21.9–37.3); the page blends the two nearest. Page grew from 0.2 to 1.5 MB.
- **Hosting: GitHub Pages** from `main` / root, with a root `index.html` that forwards to `app/pill_comparison.html` and `.nojekyll` so the journal files aren't processed.

## Findings

- **Higher BMI makes late pills dip less in the model**, for both pills, because slower clearance keeps levels higher:

  | BMI | Minipill, 3 h late | Combined, 3 h late | Minipill, 1 missed | Combined, 1 missed |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | 21.9 | 23% | 4% | 88% | 28% |
  | 30 | 20% | 3% | 83% | 21% |
  | 37.3 | 16% | 2% | 76% | 16% |

- **This is not what Edelman worried about.** Their concern is the start of each pack: obese women took about twice as long to reach steady levels (10 vs. 5 days) and had a lower first-day peak (1.9 vs. 2.5 ng/mL, not significant). Slower clearance alone can't lower the peak, so that effect isn't in the model. Said plainly on the page and in the README.
- **Combined-pill usual low rises with BMI**: 1.91 ng/mL at 21.9 → 4.06 ng/mL at 37.3.

## How the new code works

Building on what I learned on Nov 15 (`simulate`, `exact_single_dose`, the sum of exponentials).

- **`clearance_factor_for_bmi(bmi)`** (`params.py`). Turns BMI into a clearance multiplier in two lines:
  - `frac = np.clip((bmi - 21.9) / (37.3 - 21.9), 0, 1)`: how far along from the normal group to the obese group, 0 to 1. `np.clip` holds it at 0 below 21.9 and at 1 above 37.3, which is what makes it flat outside the study's range.
  - `factor = 0.491 ** frac`: a power, not a straight line, so each extra BMI point multiplies clearance by the same amount (about 4.5% less per point). Halfway (BMI 29.6) gives √0.491 = 0.70.
  - It works on single numbers and on whole NumPy arrays, which the test uses to check the curve only ever goes down.
- **`Drug.with_clearance_scaled(factor)`** (`pk.py`). `Drug` is a frozen dataclass, so its values can't be changed. `dataclasses.replace(self, k10=self.k10 * factor)` makes a **copy** with only `k10` changed. Clearance is `k10 × V_L`, so it scales exactly by `factor`; `ka`, `F`, the volumes and `k12`/`k21` stay the same, which matches Edelman's "no difference in volume of distribution".
- **`single_dose_terms(drug, dose)`** (`pk.py`). The first half of `exact_single_dose`, split out. It returns the three rates (−eigenvalues: ka, α, β) and their amplitudes, so the single-pill curve is C(t) = Σ a·e^(−r·t). `exact_single_dose` now just sums them. I needed the numbers on their own so the export script could put them in the web page.
- **Why the page treats the two pills differently.**
  - **Minipill (and the combined pill without SHBG):** the model is linear, so a month of pills is just the single-pill curve added up once per pill, shifted to each pill's time (superposition; the page's `multi()` function). The page only needs the three (rate, amplitude) pairs and can work out any pill schedule itself.
  - **Combined pill with SHBG:** clearance depends on SHBG, which changes over time, so the model isn't linear and pills can't simply be added up. Python has to run `simulate` for every scenario, and the page stores the results.
- **`export_comparison_data.py`.** Loops over `BMI_GRID = np.linspace(21.9, 37.3, 9)`. At each BMI it scales both drugs, saves their single-dose terms, and runs the combined pill with SHBG for the month and for all 50 late-pill options. Everything goes into one JSON object, `MODEL`, which a regular expression writes between the `/*DATA*/` and `/*END DATA*/` markers in the page. SHBG is saved once, because it doesn't depend on clearance.
- **Blending in the page (JavaScript).** For the BMI entered, `g = (bmi − 21.9) / step` says where it falls on the grid; `gi = floor(g)` is the grid point below, `gw = g − gi` how far towards the next one. `blend(f) = (1 − gw)·f(gi) + gw·f(gi + 1)` mixes the two nearest curves. Every BMI-dependent curve on the page goes through `blend`.
- **Why a higher BMI means smaller dips.** A longer half-life means less of the drug leaves in the hours a pill is late, so the level falls a smaller fraction below its usual low. The same idea as minipill vs. combined pill, just smaller.
- **How it's checked.** The tests confirm: the factor is 1, then 0.49, and only goes down; scaling keeps volumes and exactly halves clearance (doubling the single-dose AUC); the terms reproduce `exact_single_dose`; the obese half-life ratio is 1.75. In headless Chrome, the default 60 kg / 166 cm gives exactly the old numbers, and BMI 37 matches Python.

## Learning `clearance_factor_for_bmi` in depth

After building the BMI feature I went through the new functions. The three that matter most: `clearance_factor_for_bmi` (where the study's data enter the model), `Drug.with_clearance_scaled` (applies it: only `k10` changes) and `blend` / `updateBody` in the page (gets it onto the website). I started with `clearance_factor_for_bmi`.

**Where the 0.49 comes from.** V = CL × t½ / ln 2. Edelman found the same V in both groups, so clearance is proportional to 1 / half-life: CL_obese / CL_normal = 25.6 / 52.1 = 0.491.

**The two lines of code:**

```python
frac = np.clip((np.asarray(bmi, dtype=float) - BMI_REFERENCE) / (BMI_OBESE - BMI_REFERENCE), 0.0, 1.0)
return OBESE_CLEARANCE_RATIO ** frac
```

- `np.asarray(bmi, dtype=float)` turns the input (one number, a list or an array) into a NumPy array of decimals, so the arithmetic works on many BMIs at once. A plain Python list can't do `[25, 30] - 21.9`.
- `(bmi − 21.9) / 15.4` is where the BMI sits between the two groups: 0 at 21.9, 0.5 at 29.6, 1 at 37.3, negative below, above 1 beyond.
- `np.clip(..., 0, 1)` keeps that in 0–1, which makes the curve flat at both ends (×1 below 21.9, ×0.491 above 37.3).
- `0.491 ** frac` is the multiplier. Because it's a power, each BMI point multiplies clearance by the same 0.955 (about 4.5% less per point).

| BMI | 18 | 21.9 | 25 | 29.6 | 33 | 37.3 | 45 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| frac (clipped) | 0 | 0 | 0.20 | 0.50 | 0.72 | 1 | 1 |
| multiplier | 1 | 1 | 0.87 | 0.70 | 0.60 | 0.49 | 0.49 |

**The pattern:** `frac` says *where* the BMI is between the groups; `OBESE_CLEARANCE_RATIO` says *how big* the effect is; `**` combines them; `np.clip` only matters outside the range.

**Quiz.**

1. *What does BMI 60 return?* I said 1. It's **0.491**: 60 is above 37.3, so frac (2.47) is clipped down to 1, not up from 0. ×1 only happens at BMI 21.9 or below. I had the right idea that the clip decides it.
2. *A new study says 0.6× instead of 0.49×. What do you change?* I said the frac line. It's **`OBESE_CLEARANCE_RATIO`** (or the half-lives in the source dictionary). `frac` doesn't contain the 0.49; it only places the BMI between the groups. The function itself wouldn't change.
3. *Why `np.asarray`?* I said to turn the input into decimals. Half right: `dtype=float` does that, but it acts on the BMI passed in, not the dictionary, and it also turns lists into arrays so the maths works on many BMIs at once.
4. *If `BMI_OBESE` moved from 37.3 to 40, what happens at BMI 37.3?* I said smaller, through frac and then the clip. Right that frac changes; but frac = 15.4 / 18.1 = 0.851 is inside 0–1, so the clip doesn't apply, and 0.491^0.851 = **0.546**, **bigger**. With a number below 1, a smaller power gives a bigger result, so a lower frac always means closer to normal clearance.

**To revisit:** which way the clip pushes values above the range, and that a smaller power of a number below 1 is bigger. Next: `with_clearance_scaled`.
